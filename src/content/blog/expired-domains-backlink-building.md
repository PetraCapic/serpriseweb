An expired domain is a domain its owner stopped renewing, which anyone can then register or buy at auction. For link building, its value is the backlinks it collected while it was alive. In 2026 those links can still help your site, but only in a narrower set of cases than when we first wrote this guide: Google now has a named spam policy for expired domain abuse, and it is enforced.

This is the updated version of our guide from 2021. The checks for a good domain are mostly the same. What changed is which uses are worth the risk.

## What is an expired domain?

Every domain is registered for a period and has to be renewed. If the owner does not renew it, the domain goes through a grace and redemption period at the registry, and then becomes available again. For `.com` that whole process usually takes a little over two months from the expiry date.

People let domains expire for ordinary reasons: the business closed, a project ended, the company rebranded, the person who paid for it left or passed away. The links pointing at the domain do not disappear when the site does. A news article from 2018 that linked to a local bakery still links there in 2026.

That is the opportunity: the domain is worthless to its old owner, and its links can be valuable to someone working in the same field.

## What changed: Google's expired domain abuse policy

In March 2024 Google added three new spam policies. One of them is about expired domains. In Google's words, expired domain abuse is when an expired domain name is purchased and repurposed primarily to manipulate search rankings by hosting content that provides little to no value to users. Google's examples include affiliate content on a site that used to belong to a government agency, and casino content on a former school's site.

Two things in that definition matter for link builders:

1. **Buying an expired domain is not the problem.** Google is explicit that the policy is about the purpose and the content.
2. **The test is value to users and continuity.** A domain that keeps serving its old audience is fine. A domain bought for its links and filled with unrelated content is the target.

We like to compare it with a company acquisition. When one company buys a smaller competitor, the first thing it usually does online is redirect the competitor's domain to its own site. Nobody calls that spam, because the customers, the topic and the intent carry over. The same logic decides whether your expired domain is an asset or a liability: **does the old audience still find what it came for?**

## Is it still working? What we have seen

Back in 2021 we analysed a German car buying site that ranked for "wir kaufen dein Auto" type queries with very high search volume. Of its 1,777 backlinks, 1,334 came through 301 redirects from other domains, many of them completely unrelated to cars, with anchor texts mentioning other brands. It ranked anyway. That kind of setup was the reason Google wrote the policy, and we would not build a client's site on it today.

What still works in our experience is the boring version: a domain from the same niche, with links from real sites, rebuilt or redirected so the old content continues to exist.

## How to vet an expired domain: the checks that matter

Most expired domains are worthless for SEO because they have no meaningful links. Of those that do, many were already used and abused by other SEOs. These are the checks we run before buying anything.

### 1. Backlinks: quality, not quantity

A domain with 36 links from real articles on sites that are hard to get into is worth more than one with 1,245 links from blog comments, forum profiles and directories. Open the referring domains list in Ahrefs, Semrush or Majestic and look at the ten best ones by hand. Ask: would we be happy if this site linked to us directly?

### 2. Niche relevance

If your site is about travel, a domain that used to be a travel blog or a tourist agency is a candidate. A domain that used to be a law firm is not, even if you could write a travel post about legal documents. Relevance is also what the expired domain abuse policy looks at, so this is now the most important check.

### 3. History in the Wayback Machine

Put the domain into the [Wayback Machine](https://web.archive.org/) and go through the snapshots year by year. You want to see one consistent site that slowly faded. Red flags:

- the site suddenly turns into a "general news" blog that writes about everything and links out to random businesses;
- pages in a different language from the original site;
- casino, pharma, adult or loan content at any point;
- a parked page with ads for several years (common, not always a problem, but the links have been unused for that long).

A domain that was already used for churn-and-burn SEO is often ignored by Google or, worse, can bring problems to whatever it links to.

### 4. Anchor text profile

Look at the anchor text distribution. Natural anchors are brand names, URLs and phrases like "this article" or "the website". Lots of exact-match commercial keywords, or anchors in a language the site never used, mean someone built links to it artificially.

### 5. Where the links point

If most links point to the homepage, you can recover most of their value with one page or one redirect. If they are spread across dozens of deep URLs, you need to bring back or redirect each of those URLs individually. Export "best pages by links" and note the URLs with the strongest links; you will need that list in every scenario below.

### 6. Metrics as a filter, not a verdict

Ahrefs DR, Majestic Trust Flow and Moz DA help you throw out the bulk of junk quickly. All of them can be manipulated, and a high DR from a few spammy link sources is common on expired domains. Use metrics to shortlist, then decide by looking at the actual links and history.

### 7. Is it still indexed, and is the name clean?

Search `site:domain.com` in Google. A domain with links but zero pages indexed long after it was rebuilt is a warning sign. Also check that the domain name does not contain someone else's trademark: that can cost you the domain later regardless of SEO.

## Three ways to use an expired domain, from safest to riskiest

### 1. Rebuild the old site (lowest risk)

Restore the site as it was, using the archived pages as the base, then keep it useful: update the content, fix what is outdated, and add new pages on the same topic. Tools exist that export a site from the Wayback Machine to HTML, and there are freelancers who do it as a service.

Once the site is alive again, a natural mention of your site where it genuinely fits is reasonable. This approach is the closest to what Google describes as legitimate: the domain keeps serving its original purpose.

### 2. 301 redirect to matching pages (the strongest, if the niche matches)

A 301 redirect sends visitors and link signals from the expired domain to your site. Done well, it is the most efficient way to use a strong, relevant domain.

- **Redirect page by page**, not everything to your homepage. Map the old URLs with the best links to the pages on your site that cover the same thing. It is the same process as a site migration, which we describe step by step in [301 redirects in bulk](/blog/301-redirects-in-bulk/).
- **Only redirect relevant domains.** One or two strong domains from your niche can be worth more than a year of outreach. Ten unrelated ones pointed at one product page look exactly like what the policy describes.
- **Keep paying for the domain.** If the domain expires again, the redirect stops and so does the value. Put the renewals on auto-pay.

A practical note from our own redirects: some registrars and country domains (we hit this with `.hr`) do not offer simple domain forwarding, so you need minimal hosting or a CDN rule to serve the 301. A `.com` at most registrars can be forwarded without hosting, but check that the forwarding is a real server-side 301 and not a frame or a 302.

### 3. Private blog networks (high risk, we do not recommend it for client sites)

A PBN is a network of sites on expired domains that exist mainly to link to your money site. It worked for years and some people still make it work. It is also the most direct example of what Google's expired domain and link spam policies target, it needs constant maintenance (hosting, content, renewals) and when it fails, it can take the money site down with it.

If a domain is good enough to be worth that effort, rebuilding it as a real site (option 1) gets you most of the benefit with a fraction of the risk.

## Where to find good expired domains

Domains with strong backlinks rarely drop to normal registration. Their owners or the registrars put them into auctions before deletion, and backorder services try to catch them the second they drop.

- **Auctions and backorders:** [GoDaddy Auctions](https://auctions.godaddy.com/), [DropCatch](https://www.dropcatch.com/), [NameJet](https://www.namejet.com/) and SnapNames.
- **Search and filtering:** [ExpiredDomains.net](https://www.expireddomains.net/) lists deleted and pending-delete domains with filters for backlinks, age and archive history. Sign up, open "Pending Delete" and use "Show Filter" to narrow the list, then check where each domain is auctioned.
- **Pre-vetting tools:** SpamZilla adds spam checks, anchor text and archive history on top of the lists, which saves a lot of manual work.
- **Domain brokers** who use their own catching tools. You pay more, but you get domains you would not win yourself.

A domain that is available for normal registration price and still has great links is rare. Assume it was already used by someone and check twice.

## What does it cost compared with buying links?

A good niche domain at auction typically costs from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars, plus renewals and, depending on the use, hosting and content. When we started using expired domains, a single guest post on a decent site cost 200 to 400 dollars and on strong sites over 1,000. One relevant domain with a strong link profile can therefore be cheaper than the links it brings, on the condition that you vet it properly and use it in a way that is still worth something in a year.

## Summary

- Expired domains are still useful in 2026, but the value lies in relevance and continuity, not in raw link counts.
- Vet every domain: backlinks by hand, niche, Wayback history, anchors, where links point, indexation.
- Prefer rebuilding or page-level redirects of relevant domains. Avoid unrelated domains and thin content.
